IN THE SUPREME COURT OF INDIA
Criminal Appeal No. 1234 of 2024

STATE v. ACCUSED PERSON
Decided: 10 September 2024

JUDGMENT

Part I — Background and Facts

1. This appeal arises from a judgment of the High Court acquitting the respondent of charges
under Section 302 of the Indian Penal Code. The prosecution alleged that the respondent
caused the death of the deceased with premeditated intent, constituting murder within
the meaning of Section 300 IPC.

2. The prosecution relied on circumstantial evidence. The accused was found near the scene
of the crime. A forensic report confirmed the cause of death as blunt force trauma.
The accused denied involvement and claimed alibi.

Part II — Law on Murder and Culpable Homicide

Section 300. Murder.
Culpable homicide is murder if the act by which the death is caused is done with the
intention of causing death, or if the act is done with the intention of causing such
bodily injury as the offender knows to be likely to cause the death of the person to
whom the harm is caused, or if the act is done with the intention of causing bodily
injury to any person and the bodily injury intended to be inflicted is sufficient in
the ordinary course of nature to cause death.

Section 299. Culpable Homicide.
Whoever causes death by doing an act with the intention of causing death, or with the
intention of causing such bodily injury as is likely to cause death, or with the knowledge
that he is likely by such act to cause death, commits the offence of culpable homicide.

Distinction Between Murder and Culpable Homicide.
The distinction between murder and culpable homicide not amounting to murder turns on
the degree of knowledge and intention. Where the act is done with the knowledge that it
is imminently dangerous such that death is the most probable result, and there is no
justification, it amounts to murder. Culpable homicide not amounting to murder covers
cases where the intention and knowledge are of a lesser degree.

Part III — Principles of Criminal Procedure

Section 313 Examination.
Under Section 313 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, the accused has a right to be
examined by the court after the prosecution evidence is closed. The purpose is to afford
the accused an opportunity to explain circumstances appearing against him. Answers given
under Section 313 may be taken into consideration, but they are not on oath.

Right to Fair Trial.
Every accused person is entitled to a fair trial. The prosecution must prove the guilt
of the accused beyond reasonable doubt. The burden of proof rests entirely on the
prosecution. Mere suspicion, however grave, cannot substitute for proof.

Circumstantial Evidence.
When a case rests entirely on circumstantial evidence, the chain of circumstances must
be complete, consistent with the guilt of the accused, and inconsistent with innocence.
The circumstances must be of a conclusive nature and tendency. Gaps in the chain of
circumstances must benefit the accused.

Part IV — Bail and Interim Relief

Section 437. Bail in Non-Bailable Offences.
When any person accused of, or suspected of, the commission of any non-bailable offence
is arrested or detained without warrant by an officer in charge of a police station, he
may be released on bail, subject to the discretion of the court. In cases of serious
offences such as murder, bail may be denied if there are reasonable grounds for believing
the accused guilty.

Section 438. Anticipatory Bail.
Any person who has reason to believe that he may be arrested on accusation of having
committed a non-bailable offence may apply to the Session Court or High Court for
anticipatory bail. The court may, if it thinks fit, direct that such person shall,
on arrest, be released on bail.

Part V — Writ Jurisdiction

Article 32. Writ Petition to Supreme Court.
The right to move the Supreme Court by appropriate proceedings for the enforcement of
fundamental rights is guaranteed. The Supreme Court shall have power to issue directions
or orders or writs, including writs in the nature of habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition,
quo warranto, and certiorari, whichever may be appropriate.

Habeas Corpus.
A writ of habeas corpus may be issued to secure the release of a person who is unlawfully
detained. The court will examine whether the detention is in accordance with law.

Writ of Mandamus.
A writ of mandamus may be issued to compel a public authority to perform a duty imposed
on it by law which it has refused or failed to perform.

Part VI — Decision and Conclusion

Having considered the evidence on record, this Court finds that the prosecution has failed
to establish a complete chain of circumstances connecting the accused to the crime.
The benefit of doubt must be given to the accused. The acquittal by the High Court
is upheld. This appeal is accordingly dismissed.

Ordered accordingly.
