Chapter 1 — Formation of Contracts

Section 1. Essential Elements of a Valid Contract.
A valid contract requires the following essential elements: (a) offer, (b) acceptance,
(c) consideration, (d) capacity of parties, and (e) free consent. An agreement enforceable
by law is a contract. Every promise and every set of promises forming the consideration
for each other is an agreement. A proposal when accepted becomes a promise.

Section 2. Offer and Acceptance.
An offer is a proposal made by one party to another expressing willingness to enter into
a contract on certain terms. Acceptance must be absolute and unqualified. A counter-offer
constitutes rejection of the original offer. An offer may be revoked before acceptance is
communicated to the offeror.

Section 3. Consideration.
Consideration is something of value given by the promisee at the request of the promisor.
Past consideration is generally not valid unless it was given at the promisor's request.
Consideration need not be adequate but must be sufficient in the eyes of the law. A promise
to perform an existing legal duty does not constitute valid consideration.

Section 4. Capacity to Contract.
Every person is competent to contract who is of the age of majority, of sound mind, and
not disqualified from contracting by any law to which he is subject. A minor's agreement
is void ab initio. A contract entered into by a person of unsound mind is voidable.

Section 5. Free Consent.
Consent is said to be free when it is not caused by coercion, undue influence, fraud,
misrepresentation, or mistake. Where consent is caused by coercion, the contract is
voidable at the option of the party whose consent was so obtained.

Chapter 2 — Performance and Breach

Section 6. Performance of Contracts.
The parties to a contract must either perform or offer to perform their respective promises.
Performance by one party may be a condition precedent to the obligation of the other.
Time of performance, when stipulated, is of the essence of the contract.

Section 7. Breach of Contract.
When a party to a contract refuses or fails to perform his obligations, breach of contract
occurs. Breach may be actual or anticipatory. In case of anticipatory breach, the aggrieved
party may immediately sue for damages without waiting for the performance date.

Section 8. Remedies for Breach of Contract.
Upon breach, the aggrieved party is entitled to: (a) damages, (b) specific performance,
(c) injunction, and (d) quantum meruit. Damages aim to place the injured party in the same
position as if the contract had been performed. Special damages must be in the reasonable
contemplation of the parties at the time of contracting.

Section 9. Liquidated Damages and Penalty.
A sum named in a contract as the amount to be paid in case of breach is treated as
liquidated damages if it is a genuine pre-estimate of loss. A sum stipulated as a penalty
is subject to reduction by the court. Courts do not enforce penalties but award reasonable
compensation.

Chapter 3 — Specific Contracts

Section 10. Contract of Agency.
An agent is a person employed to do any act for another or to represent another in dealings
with third persons. The person for whom such act is done is called the principal. An agent
can bind his principal to contracts made within the scope of his authority.

Section 11. Contract of Guarantee.
A contract of guarantee is a contract to perform the promise or discharge the liability of
a third person in case of his default. The person who gives the guarantee is called the
surety. The surety is co-extensive in liability with the principal debtor unless agreed otherwise.

Section 12. Contract of Bailment.
A bailment is the delivery of goods by one person to another for some purpose, upon a
contract that they shall, when the purpose is accomplished, be returned or otherwise disposed
of according to the directions of the person delivering them. The bailor has a duty to
disclose known defects in the goods bailed.
